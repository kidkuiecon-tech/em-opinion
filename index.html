Global Macro Thesis

September FOMC shifted the EM setup from “local-currency carry helped by weak DXY” toward “selective carry under a firmer-USD and higher-front-end-rate regime.” UST curve has bear-flattened as front-end yields repriced Fed hikes more aggressively than the long end. In EM Asia, the curve reaction is mixed, with Indonesia and the Philippines showing a more bear-flattening pattern, while Malaysia, Thailand and Singapore are closer to bear-steepening. This matters because the previous EM local-currency return cushion came largely from FX rather than duration. If DXY now firms after the hawkish FOMC, hard-currency EM debt may regain relative appeal versus local-currency debt, not because spreads are universally cheap, but because local FX beta is less helpful and investors may prefer USD carry/spread exposure over unhedged EM FX risk.

01
Post-FOMC regime: Fed credibility restored, but the price is tighter front-end rates
Latest layer: JPM + HSBC + UBS; earlier layer: Citi, MS, Maybank, HSBC pre-FOMC

September FOMC changes the EM setup. Before the meeting, several September papers still framed the US as a long-end credibility problem: UST 10Y near 5%, concern about fiscal premium, and a debate on whether a hike could act as the antidote to long-end inflation risk. After the meeting, the latest HSBC and JPMorgan papers shift the emphasis: the Fed has delivered a hike, the dots have moved higher, and the market is no longer forced into a USD-debasement narrative.

This does not make the global backdrop clean. UBS still describes global bond yields as anchored at higher levels, with inflation shock, resilient growth and fiscal concerns raising term premium. JPMorgan argues that near- and medium-term Fed expectations explain much of the curve flattening. The implication is that EM can still function, but it is now doing so under a higher-front-end-rate regime rather than a falling-dollar tailwind.

Earlier September papers are still useful: Citi’s Fed and US Rates papers flagged the idea that a hike could restore credibility; Morgan Stanley and Maybank kept UST 5% as a key market constraint; HSBC pre-FOMC material highlighted USD inflection points. The latest 22–23 September sources confirm that this risk has moved from “possible” to “priced and tradeable.”

02
UST curve shape: bear-flatten first, not broad duration relief
Front-end repricing drives the narrative; long-end remains the risk constraint

The curve message should be written precisely. The US curve has not simply “sold off”; it has bear-flattened because the short end and belly repriced the Fed path more aggressively than the long end. This is visible in Maybank’s pre-FOMC rate snapshot and in JPMorgan’s post-FOMC forecast revisions.

This matters for EM because a bear-flattening UST shock is different from a pure long-end fiscal selloff. A front-end driven move supports USD and raises hedging costs, while still hurting EM duration. A long-end driven move would be more directly negative for duration, equities and risk appetite. September sources show both risks, but the post-FOMC evidence gives more weight to front-end Fed credibility.

For the dashboard, the curve section should not be reduced to a table. It should explain the transmission: higher front-end US yields support USD and cash, make EM local FX carry less forgiving, and shift the relative debate toward hard-currency EM debt and selected local curves rather than broad local-duration beta.

Views

JPMorgan Front-end and medium-term Fed expectations explain most of the flattening over the past month; JPM prefers hedged curve structures.

Maybank Pre-FOMC UST curve already showed 2Y +19bp versus 10Y +16bp and 30Y +11bp in one week, a bear-flattening shape.

UBS Still expects two hikes in 2026 before cuts in 2027; forward curve trades preferred over outright duration.

Morgan Stanley / Citi Long-end risk remains the cross-asset pain point, but the latest Fed delivery has shifted the debate toward credibility rather than debasement.

03
USD: from debasement fear to tactical strength
A stronger dollar becomes the central post-FOMC transmission channel

The most important change after FOMC is the USD narrative. Before the meeting, USD weakness and debasement risk were central to several EM carry arguments. Citi’s global macro work leaned into USD funding for EM carry and gold as a debasement expression. After the meeting, HSBC explicitly argues that the hawkish FOMC has freed the USD from the debasement theme.

The stronger USD thesis is not based only on rates. HSBC adds two other channels: energy prices are less damaging to the US than to Europe, the UK and Japan; and higher sovereign yields are no longer a US-only fiscal vulnerability. This combination supports DXY tactically even if the long-term fiscal debate remains unresolved.

This does not force the dashboard into a pure long-USD stance. The correct formulation is: local-currency EM return assumptions must be stress-tested against a firmer USD, and EM FX positions need a clearer country-specific carry or external-balance cushion.

Views

HSBC DXY up; sell GBP-USD trade opened; USD gains expected to be modest and tactical rather than disorderly.

JPMorgan Dollar direction remains important for EM asset performance; carry is less forgiving if the dollar stops falling.

04
Energy, El Niño and commodities: inflation risk is no longer a side issue
Oil and food risk reinforce the Fed/FX constraint rather than replacing it

September papers are unusually consistent on commodities and weather risk. Citi’s El Niño tracker and ASEAN agricultural products paper turn CPO and food inflation into a macro factor. Citi and HSBC commodity/oil papers add a separate energy shock channel. The result is that inflation risk is not just a US CPI issue; it is also a country-selection issue inside EM.

This is why the dashboard should not treat ASEAN as uniformly resilient. Thailand, Philippines and India have high food-and-energy weights, while oil-importing FX such as INR, IDR and PHP face more pressure when energy prices stay elevated. Malaysia has some commodity offset but domestic rates and MGS repricing still matter.

For portfolio construction, El Niño and oil are not only risk paragraphs. They explain why front-end policy pricing remains sticky, why local bond curves are vulnerable, and why hard-currency carry can look cleaner than unhedged local FX in some markets.

HSBC Energy prices reassert their grip on FX; EUR, GBP and JPY look vulnerable if energy remains elevated.

The September source pack still supports EM resilience, ASEAN investment themes, AI/data-center capex and selective local rates. 01
EM macro: resilience survives, conviction narrows
Latest sources do not kill EM, but they reduce broad beta conviction

The latest September evidence argues for resilience, not complacency. JPMorgan’s EM conference takeaways show investors still comfortable with EM fundamentals and yields, but conviction is not high. HSBC’s EM Sentiment Survey says bulls remain the majority, yet net bullishness has fallen and cash has risen. DB says EM local fixed income has held up better than in previous geopolitical shocks, but returns were driven by FX rather than duration.

The earlier-month papers broaden this: UBS EM Resilience, JPM EM Asia Local Markets, Maybank ASEAN, DBS ASEAN outlook and BofA EM Asia FX all support the idea that EM and ASEAN are not broken. But the 22–23 September layer adds a different conclusion: exposure should be more selective because USD, oil, front-end rates and valuation are now tighter constraints.

In practical terms, EM is still investable, but the expected return stack changes. Carry still matters. Local duration still has country-specific opportunities. But the broad “weak DXY + EM local FX” setup has weakened.

02
Local-currency debt: FX helped before; USD strength reduces that cushion
The local-vs-hard-currency debate becomes the core asset-allocation question

This is the narrative correction the dashboard needs. Earlier returns in EM local currency debt were flattered by EMFX performance during a weak-dollar phase. DB explicitly shows that Q3 unhedged EMFI returns were driven almost entirely by FX. SCBT shows that a firmer USD is already behind some decline in EM LCY gains. Therefore, if the post-FOMC USD view is stronger, the local-currency cushion becomes less reliable.

This does not mean selling all local debt. It means local debt needs more specific justifications: cheap valuation, real yield, policy-pricing gap, local liquidity, curve shape or external-balance support. DB likes selective EM local fixed income where valuations improved; SCBT highlights India, Indonesia, Korea, Thailand, Singapore and Philippines differently; JPM What’s Priced In gives the policy-pricing screen.

Hard-currency EM debt may regain relative appeal under this setup because it reduces unhedged local FX beta while preserving USD spread/carry exposure. But the report should not overstate this. JPM remains market weight EMBIGD/CEMBI/GBI-EM and DB notes spreads/valuations are not universally cheap.

03
Asia local markets: SCBT Arup becomes the anchor, not a footnote
Country-specific curve and policy pricing dominate broad regional calls

SCBT Arup Strategy should be treated as the Asia local-markets anchor because it gives the cleanest cross-country map after the rates repricing. It shows EM LCY performance wavering, policy-rate hike expectations persisting in most EM, and local-country curve reactions that are not uniform.

India is neutral because RBI has not hiked yet, liquidity withdrawal can flatten the curve, and the end of FCNR(B) inflows weighs on IGBs. Indonesia is neutral, with local liquidity supporting the front end and 5Y more than broad duration. Korea shows front-loaded BoK hikes that dampen terminal-rate pricing and a curve shift from steepening toward flattening heading into Q4. Thailand has BoT on prolonged hold, THOR outperforming amid Fed/BoT divergence, and 10Y THOR about 3 SD above fair value. Singapore faces higher US pass-through and tightening local liquidity, while MAS lower duration supply gives some offset. Philippines is most vulnerable among ASEAN peers because PHP weakness and supply pressure weigh on RPGBs.

This SCBT map also helps reconcile Maybank and JPM: Malaysia’s MGS selloff may create a better risk/reward window, but EM Asia curves cannot be bought indiscriminately. Local liquidity, FX sensitivity and front-end hike pricing matter more than regional labels.

04
What’s Priced In: policy-pricing gap becomes the valuation layer
Market pricing now carries more information than headline policy-rate calls

The dashboard should keep the “What’s Priced In” layer as agreed. JPM’s 16 Sep and 22 Sep WPI files show how EM markets price rate changes against JPM forecasts. Post-FOMC, this is more valuable because Fed repricing spills into EM front ends and money-market curves.

For September, the screen shows that several EMs price more hikes or fewer cuts than JPM forecasts, including Poland, Czech Republic, Brazil, Colombia, Mexico and parts of Asia such as Thailand, Taiwan, Korea and India. Brazil is a special case where near-term market pricing can still reflect cuts, but the gap versus JPM forecast changes by horizon.

The correct use is not to mechanically buy the largest gap. It is to explain where market pricing is already tight and where a rates trade has enough valuation cushion. That is why DB’s local FI valuation, SCBT’s country charts and JPM’s WPI must be blended rather than shown as separate boxes.

ASEAN and Thailand: investment/AI themes survive, but rates and FX matter more
AI/data center capex is supportive; it does not remove oil, USD and local-rate risk

ASEAN is not dropped from the dashboard. The earlier 16 Sep package adds ASEAN data centers, Maybank ASEAN, UBS ASEAN equity and UBS Thailand market strategy. These papers keep the structural case alive: AI/data centers, energy transition, FDI, power capacity and industrial estates.

Thailand specifically remains a selective constructive story in UBS meetings: positive sentiment, political stability, policy agenda shifting toward investment-led growth, and investor interest in data centers and PDP 2026. SCBT adds a rates-specific layer: BoT prolonged hold, bank liquidity recycling into bonds, THOR outperforming as BoT/Fed diverge, and cheap 10Y THOR valuation.

The caveat is that ASEAN FX/rates cannot ignore the global overlay. Oil and El Niño affect inflation baskets; USD strength pressures unhedged returns; and rate-hike pricing makes front ends more sensitive. This is exactly why the dashboard should put latest global/Fed/USD evidence first while retaining the full ASEAN source base.
